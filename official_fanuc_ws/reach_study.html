Reach & dexterity inside the workspace

Forty-three thousand inverse-kinematics solutions across position, tool geometry and workspace placement. The volume is almost entirely reachable. Orientation is what you actually pay for — and the bill is set by one decision nobody has made yet.

The open question

Does the robot base stand inside the chamber, or outside it?

Everything else in this study is a second-order effect. Standing the base inside the volume is worth roughly 97 litres of usable working space — about the same as the entire gain available from every other variable combined. The current layout already assumes it, but the model has never been asked to justify it.

01

Two corrections before any number counted

The first collision-aware probe returned zero reachable poses out of forty-eight. That result was correct and useless: the workspace mesh was loaded as a solid collision object, so every pose inside it collided with it by definition. Asking for reachable space inside a solid obstacle has only one answer.

The box is therefore modelled as a region of interest, not an obstacle — sampled as a volume, with collision checking covering the robot and its attached tool. That single change moved the same probe set from 0/48 to 37/48.

The solver was cleared of suspicion, not assumed innocent

KDL is a stochastic numerical solver and the configured timeout is only 5 ms, which would quietly report reachable poses as unreachable. Sweeping the timeout across a 50× range changed the result by one sample — 37/48 at 5 ms, 37/48 at 250 ms. Failures are geometry, not the solver giving up.

Three identical passes returned 35, 36 and 37. Everything below carries roughly ±4% run-to-run noise; differences smaller than that are not findings.

Position is free. Orientation is scarce.

Each sampled point is scored by how many of thirteen approach directions the tool can actually arrive from. That ratio is the dexterity index: 1.0 means the tool can come in from anywhere, a low value means the point is reachable but only down a narrow cone.

03

Bend the tool. Don't lengthen it.

Tool length and tool bend look like similar packaging freedoms. They are not remotely equivalent, and the study separates them cleanly.

150 mm of length costs 92% of the fully-dexterous volume and drags mean dexterity down by 0.17, near-linearly at about 0.058 per 50 mm. Coverage creeps up by 1.8% — the tip reaches marginally further, but every approach direction now needs the wrist held further back, so directions drop out faster than points are gained.

45° of bend costs nothing measurable. Mean dexterity across every bend angle — 0.688, 0.681, 0.683, 0.688 — sits entirely inside the ±4% noise band. Where packaging needs the tool pointed somewhere other than along the flange, an angled mount is close to free and an extension is expensive.

04

Placement, scored exhaustively

Because the box is a region rather than an obstacle, the dexterity field belongs to the robot alone and does not move when the box moves. So placement needed one sweep of the full envelope — 10,442 points — and then every candidate placement could be scored by integrating that field. A summed-area table makes each query O(1), which is what put 32,400 placements within reach instead of 32,400 separate sweeps.

Placements are ranked by usable volume: litres where dexterity ≥ 0.5. Raw reachability saturates near 100% and discriminates nothing.

05

The decision

Base inside the chamber — current layout 234 L Ranks 107 of 16,200, within 14% of the theoretical optimum. The layout instinct was right. Requires a floor penetration and seal.
Base inside, re-optimised 271 L Box origin at x −0.35, y −0.35, floor 50 mm below the base. Worth 37 L over the current position — the smallest lever on this page, and not worth reopening the layout for on its own.
Base clear, 200 mm pedestal 137 L The best buildable option with no chamber modification. Recommended if a floor penetration is unattractive — but go in knowing it forfeits 41% of the working volume.
Base clear, flush mount 114 L Leaves 21% on the table against the pedestal for no compensating benefit. Choose this only if mounting height is genuinely fixed.

The question is no longer where to put the robot — that is now answered for every case. It is whether a floor penetration is worth roughly 97 litres of usable working volume.

06

What this model does not know

Read these before committing to a number

  • The pedestal is not modelled as an obstacle. A real 200 mm pedestal will shadow some of the low, close-in space it unlocks. 137 L is an upper bound.
  • Tool length moves the TCP, not the collision mesh. The attached geometry stays the stepper motor, so long-tool reach is sound but long-tool clearance against chamber walls is not modelled.
  • The chamber has no walls here. Modelling it as a region means nothing obstructs entry. Real walls and an aperture will cut the horizontal approaches — which are precisely the directions this arm handles best.
  • 50 mm grid, 13 directions, ±4% noise. Volumes are quantised to 0.125 L cells. Treat differences under ~5 L as ties.
  • Reachability is not planability. Every result is a per-pose IK solution. A point reachable in isolation may still be unreachable by a collision-free trajectory from the current state.
